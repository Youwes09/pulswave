\title{Response of a liquid-jet-anchored oblique detonation to jet interruption, pulsing and throttling}

\author{Yuvan Senthilkumar}
\email{yuvi.shen@outlook.com}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, Austin, Texas, USA}

\begin{abstract}
A transverse liquid jet can initiate and anchor an oblique detonation wave (ODW) in an \nh\ spray, but only steady jets have been studied. Two-dimensional Eulerian--Lagrangian simulations of a jet at a jet-to-crossflow momentum ratio $J = 1$ on a 27\textdegree\ wedge examine how the wave responds when the jet is switched off, pulsed or throttled. The detonation survives switch-off for the 130\us\ simulated, although near the wall it weakens. The lower end of the front, the base, holds for 11.5--20\us, about one flow-through time behind the front, and then retreats at 0.07--0.10\mmus. About 10\us\ of the hold remains without jet liquid; the liquid left near the jet lengthens it. In these runs pulsing held the base only if each pulse was long enough: at an off-time of 6.25\us, 50\% duty holds it and 25\% duty does not. At 50\% duty, 80~kHz keeps the base within 1.2~mm of the continuous jet, while at 20--40~kHz it oscillates by 3--4~mm. At half the jet fuel, the 80~kHz pulse holds the base and a jet throttled to $J = 0.25$ loses it; a steady half-width jet holds or loses it depending on how the injection is represented. Total-pressure recovery downstream does not distinguish a held base from a lost one, and pulsing leaves more vapor unburnt. Each case is a single realization in a dense near-wall spray where the solver's vapor mass fraction exceeds unity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An oblique detonation wave (ODW) is an oblique shock wave (OSW) closely coupled with the heat release behind it. A standing ODW burns a hypersonic airstream in a short combustor at high thermal cycle efficiency, which makes the oblique detonation wave engine (ODWE) a candidate for hypersonic air-breathing propulsion \cite{Kailasanath2000,Jiang2023review,Rosato2021}. Standing ODWs have been established in shock-tunnel combustors \cite{Zhang2022exp}, including with kerosene \cite{Han2023trip}. A practical combustor needs the wave to initiate within the combustor length and to stay anchored as conditions change \cite{Jiang2023review,ZhangWen2025}.

Initiation can be controlled by the geometry, with stepped wedges \cite{Qin2018modified}, on-wedge trips \cite{Han2023trip}, bumps \cite{Zhang2026bump,Yan2026bump} and wedges adjusted to the flow \cite{Sun2023unsteady}, or by depositing energy, as with laser hot spots \cite{Xin2024laser}. Jiang et al.\ \cite{Jiang2025energy} sustained an ODW with pulsed energy deposition at 2.3--6.5\% of the mean power that continuous deposition needs; pulsing worked above a critical frequency, set by the arrival of successive detonation waves, which fell from about 50 to 8.5~kHz as the pulse energy rose. Jets offer a third route: hot jets \cite{Wang2022hotjet,Li2020hotjet,Qin2020coflow,Han2012hotjet}, cold transverse jets \cite{Han2024jet,ZhangJiang2023jet}, jet--wedge combinations \cite{Yao2023jetwedge} and transverse injection into a non-uniform inflow \cite{Ren2025mach}. Two studies used unsteady jets. A sweeping jet shortened the initiation length by 25\% \cite{Fan2024sweeping}, and an ODW induced by a brief jet behind a wall cavity settled at the cavity's trailing edge after the jet closed \cite{Liu2026cavity}. In hot-jet experiments in a premixed flow, by contrast, the ODW decoupled when the jet pressure fell below a threshold \cite{Han2012hotjet}.

Liquid fuels make both tasks harder, because droplets must break up and evaporate before the gas can react, and evaporation cools the gas behind the shock. Two-phase ODWs initiate and stabilize differently from gaseous ones \cite{Ren2018jfm,Ren2019pci}, and in \nh\ sprays the initiation length depends non-monotonically on the droplet diameter \cite{Teng2023droplet,Tian2024jfm}. Spray ODW initiation \cite{Guo2023cnf}, detachment \cite{Guo2025cnf} and the transient dynamics of gaseous \nh\ ODWs \cite{Wu2026heptane} have been simulated. A pure \nh\ spray fails to form an ODW in a pre-injection ODWE combustor at Mach~9 and 30~km \cite{Wang2025cnf}. Wang et al.\ \cite{Wang2026ast} showed that a transverse liquid \nh\ jet initiates an ODW in such a spray on an open 27\textdegree\ wedge: for $0.1 \le J \le 0.3$ the jet mainly enriches the near-wall mixture, and for $J \ge 0.4$ its OSW initiates detonation directly. ODWs also respond to unsteady and periodic inflow \cite{Yang2019jfm,Yang2021pof,Ren2021osc}. In a confined combustor with a steady jet, inlet-pressure forcing at 4 and 8~kHz moved the triple point by 3--5~mm at $J = 0.5$, and at $J = 1$ the ODW lost stabilization \cite{Wang2026confined}. The final wave can also depend on its history \cite{Liu2018hysteresis,Han2025hysteresis}.

In all of the liquid-jet work the jet is steady. To the author's knowledge, no study of an interrupted, pulsed or throttled liquid jet anchoring an ODW has been reported. Such a jet differs from pulsed energy deposition and from a gaseous jet, because it leaves liquid and fuel-rich vapor near the wall after each pulse. This paper asks how a liquid-jet-anchored ODW responds when the jet stops, restarts, is pulsed at different duty cycles and frequencies, and is throttled, and how pulsing compares with steady jets that carry the same mean fuel. Section~\ref{sec:method} gives the method, Sec.~\ref{sec:results} the results, Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion} the discussion and Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusions} the conclusions.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Governing equations and solver}

The gas phase obeys the two-dimensional, multicomponent, compressible Navier--Stokes equations with source terms for exchange with the liquid,
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}) &= S_m, \\
\frac{\partial (\rho\mathbf{u})}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}) + \nabla p - \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma} &= \mathbf{S}_F, \\
\frac{\partial (\rho E)}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot[\mathbf{u}(\rho E + p)] + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{q} - \nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{u}) &= S_e, \\
\frac{\partial (\rho Y_i)}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u} Y_i) - \nabla\cdot(\rho \mathcal{D}_i \nabla Y_i) &= \dot\omega_i + S_{Y,i}, \quad i = 1,\dots,n_s-1,
\end{align}
where $\rho$, $\mathbf{u}$, $p$, $E$ and $Y_i$ are the density, velocity, pressure, specific total energy and mass fraction of species $i$, $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ the viscous stress tensor, $\mathbf{q}$ the heat flux, $\mathcal{D}_i$ and $\dot\omega_i$ the diffusivity and chemical source of species $i$, $n_s$ the number of species, and $S_m$, $\mathbf{S}_F$, $S_e$ and $S_{Y,i}$ the interphase sources. The system is closed by the ideal-gas law, Sutherland's law for viscosity \cite{Sutherland1893} and a unity Lewis number, with N$_2$ obtained from $\sum Y_i = 1$. Chemistry follows the 44-species, 112-reaction skeletal \nh\ mechanism of Liu et al.\ \cite{Liu2004mech}. The liquid is tracked as Lagrangian parcels with Pilch--Erdman breakup \cite{Pilch1987}, Ranz--Marshall heat transfer \cite{Ranz1952} and a sphere drag law with $C_d = 0.424$ above a droplet Reynolds number of 1000 \cite{Liu1993drag}, following Wang et al.\ \cite{Wang2024breakup}, and the phases are coupled through particle-source-in-cell sources \cite{Crowe1977}. The source terms and sub-models are given in full in Ref.~\onlinecite{Wang2026ast}.

The equations are solved with the two-phase detonationFoam solver \cite{Sun2023detonationfoam,detonationFoamSoftware} on OpenFOAM-8 \cite{Weller1998}, with Kurganov--Noelle--Petrova fluxes \cite{Kurganov2001}, limited-linear reconstruction, first-order Euler time stepping and a Courant number of 0.3. These numerics, the slip gas wall and the absence of a turbulence model follow Ref.~\onlinecite{Wang2026ast}, which used its own solver built on OpenFOAM-7 \cite{Wang2024breakup}. Two choices differ: evaporation uses OpenFOAM's boiling-aware model instead of the Abramzon--Sirignano model \cite{Abramzon1989}, which OpenFOAM-8 does not provide, and droplets rebound elastically from the wall, a model Ref.~\onlinecite{Wang2026ast} does not state.

\subsection{Configuration}

The configuration is the open-wedge case of Ref.~\onlinecite{Wang2026ast} (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}): a 27\textdegree\ wedge representing the combustor of an ODWE flying at Mach~9 and 30~km. The domain is aligned with the wedge surface, with $x$ along the wall from the leading edge and $y$ normal to it. The inflow, at 28.55~kPa, 697.0~K and 2535.0~m/s, carries stoichiometric \nh\ droplets of 10\um\ at 300~K. A liquid \nh\ jet issues normally from the wall at $x = 50$~mm through a slot of width $d_j = 0.5$~mm. The jet-to-crossflow momentum ratio is $J = \rho_l u_j^2/(\rho_2 u_2^2)$, where $\rho_l$ is the liquid density and subscript 2 denotes the state behind the wedge OSW; $J = 1$ corresponds to a jet velocity $u_j = 60.83$~m/s. The jet droplets follow a Rosin--Rammler distribution \cite{Rosin1933} with size parameter 10\um, exponent 3 and range 1--30\um\ (mean 8.9\um); Ref.~\onlinecite{Wang2026ast} gives only the average diameter, so the spread is chosen here.

The case is two-dimensional with a depth of 2~mm, and all mass flows are per 2~mm of depth. Reference~\onlinecite{Wang2026ast} gives no jet mass flow; here the jet carries $\rho_l u_j d_j$ per unit depth, that of liquid filling the slot. On the 0.2~mm grid the jet occupies two wall faces (0.4~mm), so its momentum flux per unit area is 1.25 times nominal; the quoted $J$ is computed from the jet velocity. The continuous jet injects 41.4~g/s, 4.3 times the fuel the inflow spray carries into the full domain, so reducing it is not an engine fuel saving.

\subsection{Validation}\label{sec:validation}

The mechanism's thermodynamic data, as read by the solver, give $c_p$ and $h$ identical to Cantera's \cite{Cantera} between 300 and 2500~K. A constant-pressure ignition at 1400~K, 280~kPa and equivalence ratio $\phi = 1$ reaches 1700~K at 84.8\us, against 86.1\us\ in Cantera. For the premixed \nh\ ODWs of Guo et al.\ \cite{Guo2023pci} on a 30\textdegree\ wedge at $\phi = 1$ and 0.2, the post-shock temperatures are 1454 and 1603~K (1452 and 1601~K there) and the initiation lengths 21.7 and 4.1~mm (19.8 and 3.47~mm there), with the same abrupt and smooth transitions.

Three open-wedge cases of Ref.~\onlinecite{Wang2026ast} were rerun on its full 250 $\times$ 100~mm domain with 0.2~mm cells. With pre-vaporized fuel and no jet, the near-wall temperature at $x = 10$, 55 and 75~mm is within 3\% of the published values. With the droplet inflow and no jet, the near-wall gas stays at 1110--1206~K for 60\us\ without igniting, as reported. With the jet at $J = 1$, a jet-induced ODW forms at the jet and the main ODW develops downstream, as reported for $J \ge 0.4$. This run was mapped from a crop state with the jet on and kept only 35 of about 30,000 droplet parcels, so it re-loaded droplets over roughly its first 25\us\ near the jet and 110\us\ across the domain; its times are not times from a clean start. Figure~\ref{fig:validation} compares its front height, the upper edge of the region above 1900~K, with the published front, whose time is not stated. At $x = 52$--70~mm the computed front lies 0.5--1.8~mm below the published one in the full domain and 1.5--2.8~mm below it in the reduced domain used for the jet cases. At $x = 75$--120~mm the full-domain front lies 3--7~mm below and was still rising when the run ended. About 1~mm of the offset is the uncertainty of reading the published figure; the rest may come from inputs Ref.~\onlinecite{Wang2026ast} does not state: the droplet size spread, the droplet--wall model and the jet mass flow.

\subsection{Reduced domain and cases}\label{sec:cases}

The full domain ($6.25 \times 10^5$ cells) is too costly for runs of 130\us\ after a change to the jet, so the jet cases use a reduced domain of 120 $\times$ 45~mm ($1.35 \times 10^5$ cells) with the same grid, boundary conditions and jet, started from the uniform freestream. Upstream of the jet its near-wall gas matches the full domain within 2\% in temperature and 0.4\% in pressure and velocity (at 70 and 95\us, the full-domain run being later because it re-loaded its droplets). A smaller domain of 90 $\times$ 35~mm gives the same base (Sec.~\ref{sec:diagnostics}) within 0.07~mm.

Every jet setting restarts from the continuous $J = 1$ run at 70\us; that run, continued to 200\us, is the reference. Its base moves upstream from $x = 52.1$ to 51.3~mm by 110\us\ and then stays within 0.2~mm. The run is not steady: the liquid within $y < 3$~mm downstream of the jet grows from 2.7 to 7.6~mg by 200\us, so nearly all the jet liquid stays near the wall, whereas Ref.~\onlinecite{Wang2026ast} shows droplets spread further along it. All comparisons are made against the reference at equal times.

The jet is switched as a square wave of frequency $f$ and duty cycle $D$, beginning with an on-phase, with off-time $T_\mathrm{off} = (1-D)/f$ and on-time $T_\mathrm{on} = D/f$. Each on-phase carries the continuous jet's mass flow at $J = 1$, so the mean jet fuel is nominally $D$ times that of the continuous jet. Over 70--200\us\ the continuous jet injects 5.38~mg and the 80~kHz, 50\% jet 2.79~mg, 3.7\% more than half, because the window ends inside an on-phase. Two steady jets carry half the fuel. One runs at half the velocity, which lowers $J$ to 0.25. The other keeps $J = 1$ on half the width, a single 0.2~mm wall face, with the parcels injected either into a cone from a one-cell disc on that face (cone injection) or uniformly over the face (patch injection, on a remapped mesh). Each injects 2.69~mg over 70--200\us.

Further cases run in a crop of 100 $\times$ 25~mm around the jet, branched at 50\us\ from a continuous crop run. Its upper boundary cuts the front near $x \approx 70$~mm, so the crop is used only for the base, and since it has no continuous run beyond 50\us\ its cases are compared with their position at the branch and with each other. Table~\ref{tab:cases} lists all cases. Restarting a run reproduces its base within one cell, but the attempt to change the random sequence of the droplet sub-models failed, so every case is a single realization.

\subsection{Diagnostics}\label{sec:diagnostics}

The front is found along lines of constant $y$ (1--6~mm in steps of 1~mm, and 9--38~mm in steps of 3--4~mm) as the first pressure rise by a factor of at least 1.5 over 0.8~mm with burnt gas (CO + CO$_2$ mass fraction above 0.02) within 1~mm behind it. The base is the median of the front positions at $y = 4$, 5 and 6~mm; lower down, the front is distorted by the jet bow shock and by flares along the wall.

The hold time after switch-off is the time to the first save at which the base lies more than 1.5 cells (0.3~mm) further downstream than at switch-off. For the switch-off at 70\us\ the base is measured relative to the reference at equal times, because the reference still moves upstream until 110\us; at 165\us\ and in the crop it is measured from its own position. The retreat speed is the least-squares slope of the base over the following 20\us. Both are given as ranges over thresholds of 1.25--2.5 cells.

The pulsed and equal-fuel cases fall into three groups by the largest excursion of the base from the reference: held (within about 2~mm throughout), oscillating (back and forth by 3--4~mm without a steady retreat) and lost (steady retreat to more than 8~mm behind by 200\us).

The wall layer is the liquid within $y < 3$~mm at $x = 49.5$--100~mm, located by the $x$ below which 5, 25 and 50\% of its mass lies. The plug is the part of the wall layer at $x < 52.5$~mm.

Total-pressure recovery and burnt fuel are measured on the plane $x = 100$~mm, $y < 25$~mm, which the stream processed by the jet crosses. Recovery is the mass-flux-weighted total pressure across the plane divided by that entering the domain; each cell's total pressure is the isentropic stagnation pressure at frozen composition, which matches Cantera to 0.005\%. Burnt fuel is the fuel-equivalent carbon in CO and CO$_2$ crossing the plane, reported with the unburnt \nh\ vapor. Gas cooled by evaporation without burning can have a frozen total pressure above the inflow value, so a save in 150--200\us\ is used only if at least 95\% of the mass flux lies in cells with positive flux and a total pressure below half the inflow value. The reference scatters from save to save by 0.002 in recovery and 0.02~g/s in burnt fuel (standard deviation over 150--200\us).

\subsection{Numerical sensitivity}\label{sec:robust}

The response to switch-off was computed in the crop on grids of 0.2, 0.15 and 0.1~mm (Fig.~\ref{fig:grid}). On the 0.15~mm grid the base stays within 0.55~mm of the 0.2~mm base. The 0.1~mm grid, mapped from the 0.2~mm crop 15\us\ before switch-off, gives the same hold and retreat but faster: the base moves 2.0~mm in the first 25\us\ instead of 1.4~mm, holds for 8--10\us\ instead of 12--15\us, and retreats at 0.093--0.099\mmus\ instead of 0.082--0.085\mmus. The displacement after switch-off is therefore uncertain by about 40\%. Only the jet-off case was run on the finer grids. The crop reproduces the reduced-domain base within 0.6~mm at equal times after switch-off.

Changing the droplet--wall model (rebound with restitution 0.3 and friction 0.5, or droplets that stick) keeps the base within 0.4~mm to 90\us; a no-slip gas wall keeps it within one cell to 90\us, and three to four times as many parcels at the same mass flows do so to 70\us. Restarting a run from one of its saves reproduces its base within 0.2~mm, checked for two runs. The restarted fields are not bit-identical, so this is only a lower bound on the scatter between realizations.

The liquid near the wall is dense. Over $x = 49.5$--80~mm, $y < 3$~mm it occupies 2.8\% of the volume, but it is concentrated in 250 wall cells, where it occupies 26\% of the volume at 18 times the gas mass, far outside the dilute limit of point-particle spray models \cite{Elghobashi1994}. These cells show a numerical defect: the \nh\ vapor mass fraction exceeds 1, and N$_2$ is then negative. Of 35 scanned run segments (all but the duty-sweep, 0.5\us-save and 0.1~mm runs), 27 show it, in up to 42 cells per save in the reference and up to 224 in the patch-injected half-width jet and up to 177 in the half-velocity jet, where the largest value, 7.2, occurs. All such cells lie below $y = 3.5$~mm, so the base is never measured in them. On the performance plane they occur in three runs and carry at most 0.12~g/s of mass flux; they are left out of the fuel flows. The gas state of the wall layer, including the plug, is nonetheless not reliable.

The upper front reaches the upper boundary of the reduced domain after 135--150\us; in the jet-off and 20~kHz runs the meeting point then moves upstream, to $x \approx 95$~mm by 200\us. The subsonic regions behind the front stay below $y \approx 30$~mm and are separated from that boundary by supersonic flow, so the boundary cannot influence the base, and its shocked part is excluded from the inflow total pressure.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The jet-anchored front}\label{sec:baseline}

The base sits at the foot of the jet-induced shock (Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline}). The gas approaching the jet along the wall carries an \nh\ vapor mass fraction of 0.086, and its ignition delay of at least 1899\us\ is far longer than its 24\us\ transit from the leading edge, so it does not ignite upstream. At the jet the front at $y = 1$--4~mm is a shock of pressure ratio 6.1--8.9, behind which the ignition delay falls to 0.11--0.70\us. Behind the front the gas moves at 1080--1840~m/s at $y = 3$--15~mm and crosses the first 20~mm in 11--19\us; this is the flow-through time used below.

(b) Temperature with jet and freestream droplet parcels (dots, 6000 drawn at random); the blue contour is $M = 1$. (c) \textit{n}-C$_7$H$_{16}$ vapor mass fraction.}\label{fig:baseline}

Behind the jet the liquid forms a cold, fuel-rich layer along the wall. At 80\us\ the gas within 1~mm of the wall is at least 20\% \nh\ vapor from the jet to the outflow; on average it is at 704~K with a vapor mass fraction of 0.48 and almost no oxygen, and its first 13~mm are subsonic. Within 30~mm of the jet and 3~mm of the wall lie 3.1~mg of liquid with a mass-weighted diameter of 5.8\um. Reference~\onlinecite{Wang2026ast} reports about 500~K along its near-wall streamline for $J \ge 0.5$, so the layer is a known difference from that study. The liquid barely evaporates: with the jet off it falls only from 2.72 to 2.61~mg between 5 and 25\us\ after switch-off, consistent with a $d^2$-law lifetime of about 230\us\ for a 5\um\ droplet at the observed droplet temperature of 399~K and the 418~K of the gas around the droplets (Spalding number 0.59) \cite{NIST,Spalding1953}.

\subsection{Switching the jet off}\label{sec:off}

The detonation survives the loss of its jet to the end of the run (Figs.~\ref{fig:off}(a) and~\ref{fig:offsnap}). The base holds, then moves downstream, reaching $x = 59.9$~mm at 200\us\ while the reference base stays at 51.1--51.5~mm. The front higher up moves less: at $y = 15$~mm it stays within 1.2~mm of its position until 160\us. Near the wall the front weakens; at the base heights its pressure ratio falls from 7--10 to 4.4--4.9 after 145\us, while it stays at 7--9 in the reference. Whether the lower front is still a coupled detonation after that time is not established.

The base holds for 11.5--20\us\ and then retreats at 0.07--0.10\mmus\ (Fig.~\ref{fig:off}(b)). The difference between the two switch-off times exceeds the save interval, so the hold depends on the state at switch-off. The hold is of the same order as the flow-through time of 11--19\us.

The liquid left at the jet lengthens the hold, but only part of the hold depends on it. With all jet liquid removed at switch-off, the base holds for 10\us\ instead of 15--20\us\ and moves 3.4~mm in the first 20\us\ instead of 0.8~mm. Removing only the plug in the crop gives a similar result: a hold of 10\us\ instead of 15\us\ and a displacement of 2.8~mm instead of 1.0~mm. The hold differences are one to two save intervals; the displacement differences are 9--13 cells. The base starts to move before the plug is gone. The plug holds 1.13~mg at switch-off and 0.71~mg 10\us\ later, and empties 20--25\us\ after switch-off (Fig.~\ref{fig:edge}(a)). Removing droplets that reach the wall leaves the base unchanged, but that run ends within the hold.

The base does not follow the wall layer as a whole (Fig.~\ref{fig:edge}(a)). The layer's 25\% point moves at 0.157\mmus, twice as fast as the base. The data show that the liquid near the jet delays the retreat, but not by what process. After the base has moved, the cold gas of the wall layer spreads behind the front. With the jet on it is confined below $y = 0.9$~mm. By 55\us\ after switch-off, gas below 600~K covers $x = 60$--90~mm up to $y = 13$~mm, with an \nh\ vapor mass fraction of 0.61, too rich to burn (Fig.~\ref{fig:offsnap}(d)).

The run ends 30\us\ after switch-off, only two widths were run, and the per-area momentum flux also differs (1.0 against 1.25 times nominal), so the effect of jet size is not isolated.

When the jet is switched on again at 95\us, after 25\us\ off, the base keeps moving downstream for about 15\us\ and then stops 3.0--3.2~mm behind the reference. It shows no sign of return within the 35\us\ simulated (Fig.~\ref{fig:off}(a)).

\subsection{Duty cycle}\label{sec:duty}

At a fixed off-time of 6.25\us, shorter than every hold, the duty cycle decides the outcome (Fig.~\ref{fig:duty}(a)). At 50\% duty the base is held; at 25\% it retreats as with the jet off. At 50\% duty (80~kHz) it stands 0.8~mm downstream of its starting position 40\us\ after the branch. At 37.5\% (100~kHz) it follows the 50\% case for 20\us\ and then falls back by 2~mm. At 25\% (120~kHz) it ends at $x = 55.1$~mm, near the jet-off base at 54.7~mm. Shorter and longer off-times at 25\% duty, 4.7 and 9.4\us, give the same result (Fig.~\ref{fig:duty}(b)). Liquid near the jet does not by itself hold the base: at 25\% duty and 80~kHz the wall layer gains 7\% of its liquid over 40\us, yet its 25\% point moves downstream at 0.127\mmus\ (Fig.~\ref{fig:edge}(b)).

\subsection{Pulse frequency at 50\% duty}\label{sec:pulsed}

At 50\% duty the base is held at 80~kHz and oscillates at 20--40~kHz (Fig.~\ref{fig:pulsed}). At 80~kHz it lies at most 1.2~mm behind the reference and shows no drift over 150--200\us. At 20, 30 and 40~kHz the off-times of 12.5--25\us\ are as long as the hold or longer. The base falls 3.0--4.2~mm behind the reference and returns, ending 0.6--2.2~mm behind at 200\us, against 8.6~mm with the jet off. The returns upstream are abrupt, 1.0--1.8~mm within one save, and two of the three occur during off-phases. The runs cover only 2.6 cycles at 20~kHz and 3.9 at 30~kHz, so they do not show whether the oscillations are bounded or define a critical frequency.

\subsection{Pulsed and steady jets at equal fuel}\label{sec:equalfuel}

At half the jet fuel, the 80~kHz pulse holds the base and the throttled jet loses it (Fig.~\ref{fig:equalfuel}). At half velocity ($J = 0.25$) the base ends 9.2~mm behind the reference at 200\us, close to the jet-off base, although a reacting front remains. With patch injection the base is held, ending 2.0~mm behind the reference and about 1~mm behind the 80~kHz base. With cone injection it drifts slowly until 145\us\ and is then lost, ending 10.8~mm behind. The patch-injected and half-velocity runs also have the most cells with a vapor mass fraction above 1 (Sec.~\ref{sec:robust}).

\subsection{Performance on the measurement plane}\label{sec:performance}

Recovery on the plane $x = 100$~mm does not separate a held base from a lost one (Table~\ref{tab:performance}, Fig.~\ref{fig:performance}). In the reference the stream processed by the jet loses 88\% of its total pressure by the plane. Over 150--200\us\ every half-fuel jet exceeds the reference by 0.006--0.031, including those whose bases are lost. The pulsed cases scatter by up to 0.06 from save to save, so their differences are not distinguishable from zero. This is plausibly because a weaker jet shock and a front further downstream both raise recovery. Recovery on the plane responds to a change at the jet only after 35--50\us.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The hold after switch-off has two parts. About 10\us\ remains without any jet liquid, and the liquid near the jet adds 5--10\us\ and cuts the early displacement from about 3 to 1~mm. The total, 11.5--20\us, is comparable to the 11--19\us\ in which gas crosses the first 20~mm behind the front. This is a comparison of order, not a mechanism: the hold also depends on the state at switch-off and on the grid.

In these runs, holding the base under pulsing needed two conditions: an off-time shorter than the hold, and on-phases long enough. For pulsed jets in crossflow the second is expressed by the stroke ratio $L/d = u_j T_\mathrm{on}/d_j$, which with the duty cycle governs the jet structure \cite{Johari2006,Sau2010,Karagozian2010}. At the off-time of 6.25\us\ the base is held at $L/d = 0.76$ (50\% duty), held only briefly at 0.46 (37.5\%) and not held at 0.19--0.38 (25\%). At 20--40~kHz the on-phases are long ($L/d = 1.5$--3.0), but the off-phases are as long as the hold, and the base oscillates. Its abrupt returns, mostly during off-phases, suggest that the response is not a simple delay of the forcing.

The survival of the front without its jet contrasts with the hot-jet experiments of Han et al.\ \cite{Han2012hotjet}, in which the ODW decoupled when the jet weakened. There a hot gaseous jet initiated the wave in a premixed flow by heat; here a cold liquid jet initiates it through its shock and leaves fuel-rich liquid and vapor near the wall. The cavity-jet ODW of Liu et al.\ \cite{Liu2026cavity} also outlasted its jet, held by the cavity edge. In pulsed energy deposition \cite{Jiang2025energy}, each pulse initiates a new detonation and the critical frequency is set by how soon successive waves arrive; here the detonation persists through the off-phase, and what must be bridged is the hold at the base.

At half the fuel, pulsing at 80~kHz kept the base and throttling to $J = 0.25$ did not. A fixed injector throttled to half its flow reaches exactly that value, because $J$ falls as the square of the flow, and $J = 0.25$ lies below the range $J \ge 0.4$ in which the jet's shock initiates the ODW directly \cite{Wang2026ast}. A throttled jet at $J = 0.4$--0.5 was not run, and the comparison rests on one frequency, one duty and single realizations. The two half-width jets, both at $J = 1$, differ in outcome, so the result depends on how a narrow jet is represented, not on $J$ alone. Pulsing brings no gain on the plane downstream: recovery does not change beyond the scatter, and unburnt vapor grows as the frequency falls.

Four limits bound these conclusions. First, the switching is an idealized square wave; 80~kHz at 50\% duty means a Strouhal number $f d_j/u_j = 0.66$, faster than common liquid injectors switch, so the result indicates what a jet of this size needs rather than a design point. Second, the liquid that lengthens the hold sits where the spray is dense and the solver's vapor mass fraction exceeds 1; a wall-film model, a voidage-corrected drag or a bounded species treatment could change it. Third, the simulations are two-dimensional and laminar with a slip wall, and the displacement after switch-off changes by about 40\% between the 0.2 and 0.1~mm grids. Fourth, every case is a single realization: the restart scatter of 0.2~mm is only a lower bound, while two representations of the same half-width jet end 9~mm apart. Differences within a group are therefore not resolved, and only the groups themselves, held, oscillating and lost, are used.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

Two-dimensional Eulerian--Lagrangian simulations of an \nh\ spray ODW anchored by a transverse liquid jet at $J = 1$, each case a single realization in a dense near-wall spray, give the following results.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The detonation survives the loss of its jet for the 130\us\ simulated, although its lower part weakens. The base holds for 11.5--20\us\ on the 0.2~mm grid (8--10\us\ on the 0.1~mm grid), of the order of the flow-through time behind the front, and then retreats at 0.07--0.10\mmus. Near the wall the front weakens to a pressure ratio of 4--5 after 75\us\ off.
\item About 10\us\ of the hold remains without jet liquid; the liquid near the jet adds 5--10\us. A jet twice as wide may hold longer, but the difference is one save and jet size is not isolated. A jet that returns after 25\us\ off stops the base 3~mm behind the reference.
\item At an off-time of 6.25\us, the base is held at 50\% duty ($L/d = 0.76$) and not held at 25\% duty ($L/d = 0.19$--0.38). At 50\% duty, 80~kHz holds the base within 1.2~mm of the continuous jet, while 20--40~kHz lets it oscillate by 3--4~mm.
\item At half the jet fuel, the 80~kHz pulse holds the base and a jet throttled to $J = 0.25$ loses it by 9~mm; a steady half-width jet holds it with patch injection and loses it with cone injection.
\item Total-pressure recovery 50~mm downstream does not distinguish a held base from a lost one. The pulsed jets burn up to 0.41~g/s less fuel there than the continuous jet and leave 3 to 13 times its unburnt vapor, more at lower frequency.
\end{enumerate}
